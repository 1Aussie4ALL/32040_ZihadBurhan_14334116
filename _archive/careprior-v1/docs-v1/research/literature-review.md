# Literature Review: Hybrid AI and Deterministic-Rule Clinical Intake Prioritisation

**Project:** CarePrior — UTS 32040 Industry Project, Master of IT, Spring 2026
**Author:** Zihad Burhan (solo developer)
**Date:** August 2026

## Abstract

CarePrior is a prototype clinical intake and appointment-prioritisation system for primary care. It combines free-text symptom capture interpreted by a swappable AI provider, an adaptive follow-up questioning engine, a deterministic and versioned safety-rule engine, and a mandatory human review step, all recorded to an append-only audit log. This literature review situates that design against four adjacent bodies of work: (1) existing medical appointment booking and scheduling systems, largely logistics-only and clinically inert; (2) established clinical triage frameworks (Manchester Triage System, Emergency Severity Index, Australasian Triage Scale), the deterministic, explainable precedent for CarePrior's safety-rule design; (3) AI-based symptom-checker and chatbot triage tools, whose published evaluations show persistent accuracy and safety limitations; and (4) the explainable AI (XAI) and human-in-the-loop literature, explaining why mandatory, visible human review is a safety mechanism rather than a bureaucratic add-on. A fifth section examines adaptive versus static questionnaires. The review concludes that no widely documented system combines never-overridable deterministic safety rules, AI-assisted adaptive questioning, mandatory explainable human review, and a full audit trail specifically for appointment-intake prioritisation (as distinct from diagnosis) — the gap CarePrior targets. Where evidence was thin, general, or informal rather than peer-reviewed, this is flagged explicitly so the reader can judge the strength of each claim.

## 1. Existing Medical Appointment Booking and Scheduling Systems

The dominant category of digital tooling in primary-care access is scheduling infrastructure: systems whose job is to move a patient from "wants an appointment" to "has a booked slot" as efficiently as possible, with no attempt to reason about clinical urgency.

**Consumer-facing marketplaces.** Zocdoc is the best-documented example of a direct-to-consumer booking marketplace, aggregating provider calendars and letting patients self-select an appointment type, often filtered by insurance and location. Zocdoc's own commentary acknowledges that when patients self-select the wrong appointment type or provider ("wrong-fit bookings"), the burden of catching this falls on practice staff after the fact — described in Zocdoc's own materials as unpaid clinical triage work pushed onto providers (Zocdoc, "Wrong-Fit Patients on Zocdoc: Causes and Controls," n.d.) [https://www.zocdoc.com/blog/facts/wrong-fit-bookings-lead-quality/]. This is a vendor source rather than peer-reviewed evidence, but the structural point it illustrates is architectural, not anecdotal: Zocdoc and similar marketplaces optimise for calendar-slot matching, not clinical urgency assessment, and no severity signal from the patient's free-text reason for visit feeds into any prioritisation logic.

**Public health-system referral and booking infrastructure.** The NHS e-Referral Service (e-RS) in England replaced the earlier "Choose and Book" system and lets patients and GPs select and book first outpatient appointments, by phone, in person, or online (NHS England, "e-Referrals," n.d.) [https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/e-referrals/]. e-RS's own documentation on "Appointment slot issues" describes a persistent operational problem: when no bookable slot exists at the time of search, cases enter a manual resolution process, and patients under time pressure are documented as sometimes accepting the first available appointment rather than the clinically or logistically preferable one (NHS England Digital, "Managing and Minimising Appointment Slot Issues," n.d.) [https://digital.nhs.uk/services/e-referral-service/document-library/managing-and-minimising-appointment-slot-issues]. Notably, e-RS's own launch was criticised for going live with 33 known unresolved issues without a full public beta, against the advice of the UK Government Digital Service (Sooraj Shah, "NHS e-Referral system goes live, despite 33 issues and GDS warning," *Computer Weekly*, 2015) [https://www.computerweekly.com/news/4500248131/NHS-e-Referral-system-goes-live-despite-33-issues-and-GDS-warning]. This is a decade-old report about a since-matured system, included here as a rare piece of independent published scrutiny of a national booking system's rollout risk; the underlying architectural pattern it describes — a slot-matching engine with referral triage handled separately and manually by clinicians before the booking step — persists in e-RS today. Clinical prioritisation is not automated: it is performed by the referring or receiving clinician outside the booking tool itself.

**Enterprise EHR-integrated portals.** Epic MyChart and Cerner (Oracle Health) HealtheLife are the two dominant EHR-tethered patient portals in the US market, both offering self-service scheduling, e-check-in, and messaging. Comparative technical write-ups note that portal scheduling logic is deliberately constrained to prevent patients from booking outside pre-defined scheduling rules — e.g. no overbooking, no booking into blocked or non-patient-facing slots (Epic, "Appointment Scheduling," n.d.) [https://www.epic.com/software/appointment-scheduling/]. These are rule-based systems, but the rules are administrative (calendar capacity, slot types), not clinical (symptom severity, differential urgency). Grey-literature comparative sources additionally suggest adoption limitations are as much organisational (staff workflow, change management) as technical — a lower-strength, non-peer-reviewed finding included for context only.

**Synthesis.** Across consumer marketplaces, national referral infrastructure, and enterprise EHR portals, the consistent pattern is: sophisticated calendar and logistics engineering, but no embedded clinical reasoning about which patient should be seen first. Prioritisation, where it happens at all, is performed by a human (GP, referral triage nurse, receptionist) working from the free-text reason for visit, off-system, with no structured decision support, no explainability requirement, and — critically for CarePrior's audit-trail design — typically no systematic, queryable record of *why* one patient's case was treated as more urgent than another's. This is the first half of the gap CarePrior addresses: existing booking systems are functionally correct as scheduling tools but clinically inert.

## 2. Clinical Triage Frameworks as a Reference Point for Deterministic Prioritisation

A second, largely separate literature exists around structured emergency-department triage scales. These are directly relevant to CarePrior not because CarePrior is an emergency triage tool (it explicitly is not — CarePrior addresses primary-care appointment prioritisation, not emergency dispatch), but because they are the strongest available precedent for a deterministic, versioned, explainable rule set that assigns urgency from structured clinical inputs, and whose outputs are trusted precisely because they are not silently overridable by discretion.

**Manchester Triage System (MTS).** Developed in Manchester, UK, in the 1990s in response to documented inconsistency in ad hoc ED triage decisions, MTS uses a flow-chart ("presentation") structure in which discriminators collected from the patient are deterministically translated into one of five urgency categories. A prospective observational study found MTS validity "moderate to good," weakest in young and elderly patients ("Validity of the Manchester Triage System in emergency care," *PMC*) [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5289484/]. A more recent comparative study against ESI in predicting critical events found a statistically significant, monotonic association between MTS category and critical-event occurrence [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12751399/]. The key architectural property for CarePrior is that MTS is deterministic by design: the same discriminators reliably produce the same category, which is what makes the tool auditable and defensible in a clinical-governance sense.

**Emergency Severity Index (ESI).** ESI is a five-level triage acuity scale widely used in US emergency departments, combining patient acuity with anticipated resource needs. Reliability and validity work reports strong correlation of ESI level with hospitalisation, ED length of stay, and mortality, and better validity than a simpler three-tier system ("Validity and Reliability of Emergency Severity Index and Conventional Three-Tier Triage System," *PMC*) [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7409571/]. The ESI literature is candid about error rates: misclassification can occur in up to roughly one-third of cases, with average under-triage around 10.7% and over-triage around 6.2% (Tanabe et al., "Refining Emergency Severity Index Triage Criteria") [https://scholarlyworks.lvhn.org/emergency-medicine/449/]. ESI Version 5 was explicitly revised to reduce low-acuity under-triage by emphasising abnormal vital signs [https://www.jem-journal.com/article/S0736-4679(25)00288-4/fulltext] — instructive for CarePrior, since even a mature, decades-old deterministic scale is periodically revised as real-world mistriage is measured and fed back, justifying CarePrior's treatment of its own safety rules as versioned artefacts rather than a fixed, one-off ruleset.

**Australasian Triage Scale (ATS).** ATS, the Australian and New Zealand analogue endorsed by the Australasian College for Emergency Medicine, is described in NSW Agency for Clinical Innovation evidence-check literature as correlating with resource utilisation, admission rates, and mortality (NSW ACI, "Evidence check: Emergency department triage models," 2024) [https://aci.health.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/973789/ACI-Evidence-check-ED-triage-models.pdf]; official category descriptors are published by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care [https://www.safetyandquality.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-04/emergency_triage_education_kit_-_australasian_triage_scale_-_descriptors_for_categories.pdf]. As with MTS and ESI, evidence is not uniformly positive: inter-rater reliability has been reported as only slight to moderate (κ ≈ 0.25–0.45), weakest in paediatric, psychiatric, and elderly trauma populations, with both under-triage (gastrointestinal bleeding, geriatric trauma) and over-triage (anxiety-driven presentations) documented in specific subpopulations ("Comparison of Reliability and Validity of the Chinese Four-Level and Three-District Triage Standard and the Australasian Triage Scale," *PMC*) [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6885288/].

**Relevance to CarePrior's design.** Three properties recur across MTS, ESI and ATS and map directly onto CarePrior's rule-engine decisions: (a) urgency is derived from a small set of structured discriminators via explicit, publishable logic rather than gestalt judgement — what CarePrior's IF/AND/OR safety rules replicate for symptoms such as chest discomfort combined with sweating or arm radiation; (b) even well-validated deterministic scales have known, quantified error rates and are treated as living, versioned artefacts subject to revision (mirrored in CarePrior's requirement that rules be versioned, not hard-coded once); and (c) none of these frameworks claim to replace clinician judgement — they structure and accelerate it, the precedent for CarePrior's non-negotiable human-review step. Where CarePrior differs is scope and setting: MTS/ESI/ATS are applied face-to-face by a trained triage nurse at ED arrival, not to unsupervised free-text self-report submitted remotely before any clinician contact — the harder, less-precedented problem CarePrior's adaptive-questioning layer is designed to mitigate.

## 3. AI/Chatbot-Based Symptom Checkers and Triage Tools

This is the literature area with the most direct, quantified evidence — and the evidence is largely cautionary, which is directly relevant to CarePrior's design decision that AI must never independently issue or upgrade a clinical outcome.

**Foundational accuracy audit.** The landmark study in this space is Semigran, Linder, Gidengil and Mehrotra's 2015 BMJ audit of 23 publicly available English-language symptom checkers, using standardised clinical vignettes. It found the correct diagnosis was listed first in only 34% of cases, and appropriate triage advice was given only 57% of the time (Semigran et al., *BMJ*, 2015) [https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Evaluation-of-symptom-checkers-for-self-diagnosis-Semigran-Linder/f7b90d0542b9f1319815a722c013e4090e5ac645]. A five-year follow-up study found essentially no improvement: median triage accuracy was 55.8% in 2020 versus 59.1% in 2015 (*PMC*) [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9131144/]. This stubborn resistance to improvement, despite advancing underlying AI models, is one of the strongest pieces of evidence supporting CarePrior's decision not to let an AI interpretation layer independently determine or downgrade clinical priority.

**Ada Health.** Ada is among the more rigorously evaluated commercial symptom checkers. An emergency-department observational study found Ada achieved 70% sensitivity for the correct diagnosis within its top-5 suggestions, comparable to the mean sensitivity of three independent physicians (68.9%); treating physicians rated Ada's triage decisions "fully agree" 62% of the time and "safe but too cautious" (over-triage) 24% of the time (JMIR mHealth and uHealth, 2022) [https://mhealth.jmir.org/2022/9/e38364/]. A later comparison study found Ada's top-1 diagnosis match rate was 30%, versus 40% for both ChatGPT-3.5 and WebMD and 47% for physicians in the same cohort — Ada was not uniformly superior to general-purpose LLMs or physicians on this metric (*PMC*, 2023) [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10582809/]. A 2024 randomised, crossover, double-blinded comparison of Ada against Symptoma found Ada significantly more accurate and usable than its comparator, but still found "a substantial number of potentially life-threatening diagnoses were missed by both symptom checkers" (*JMIR*, 2024) [https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e56514]. Taken together, these are among the best-evaluated tools in the category, and they still miss life-threatening presentations at a non-trivial rate — direct support for CarePrior's rule that low AI confidence or contradictory input must route to mandatory human review rather than a default low-urgency outcome.

**Babylon Health / GP at Hand.** Babylon's chatbot-driven triage service, integrated into the UK NHS-commissioned GP at Hand service, is the most publicly documented real-world *safety incident* in this literature, as distinct from a controlled accuracy study. A clinician (publicly identified as Dr David Watkins, initially posting pseudonymously) raised repeated, documented concerns that the chatbot gave unsafe triage advice, including a case in which an obese, heavy-smoking 48-year-old presenting with chest pain was advised to book a routine consultation "in the next few hours" rather than being escalated for emergency assessment (*TechCrunch*, 2021) [https://techcrunch.com/2021/03/05/uks-mhra-says-it-has-concerns-about-babylon-health-and-flags-legal-gap-around-triage-chatbots/]. The same article reports the UK medical device regulator (MHRA) formally noted concerns about the chatbot's diagnostic/triage accuracy and flagged an at-the-time gap in the regulatory framework specific to triage chatbots. Trade-press coverage additionally documents Babylon's public response to the whistleblower as combative, including a press release publicly analysing his account activity (*Patient Safety Learning* and *Mobihealthnews*) [https://www.pslhub.org/blogs/entry/583-babylon-health-lashes-out-at-doctor-who-raised-ai-chatbot-safety-concerns/] [https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/chatbot-babylon-fires-back-twitter-critic-publicly-analyzing-his-search-data]. This case is unusually well-documented for the category and is treated here as a real-world illustration rather than a controlled study; the chest-pain-under-triage example maps almost exactly onto the cardiac-warning-sign scenario that CarePrior's own "chest discomfort + sweating/arm radiation ⇒ Immediate Human Review" rule is designed to catch deterministically, so that such a case cannot be silently downgraded by an AI-only judgement call.

**NHS 111 online.** As the UK's national self-triage tool, NHS 111 online is formally classified as a Class 1 medical device (NHS England Digital) [https://digital.nhs.uk/services/nhs-111-online/nhs-111-online-is-a-class-1-medical-device], and has been the subject of a NIHR-funded mixed-methods evaluation programme [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK575176/]. A related telephone-triage accuracy study (for suspected COVID-19) found 74.2% sensitivity and 61.5% specificity for predicting serious adverse outcomes [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9644662/]. A recent systematic review comparing online symptom-assessment applications, LLMs, and laypeople for self-triage decisions found accuracy across tools ranging widely from 11.5% to 90.0%, characterising the overall evidence base on patient safety and accuracy as "weak and variable" (*npj Digital Medicine*, 2025) [https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01566-6]. The same evidence identifies a systematic directional bias: these tools tend to be risk-averse and over-triage more often than under-triage, reducing but not eliminating dangerous under-triage, at the cost of unnecessary care-seeking and system load.

**Buoy Health, K Health, and comparative evaluations.** A JMIR AI clinical-vignette study using 400 physician-approved vignettes compared several commercial symptom checkers (Buoy, K Health, Ada, Babylon, WebMD, and newer entrant Avey), finding substantial performance variance and no single tool close to physician-level accuracy across the full set (JMIR AI, 2024) [https://ai.jmir.org/2024/1/e46875]. Secondary commentary on Buoy Health's own operational data (roughly 158,000 consultations) reports about 32% of patients stated they would subsequently seek a *lower* urgency level of care than recommended, versus 4% seeking a *higher* level — a large gap between machine-recommended and patient-intended care-seeking behaviour that could not be verified against a primary peer-reviewed source in this review and is flagged as a press-summarised figure of moderate reliability.

**Synthesis and explicit sourcing caveat.** Across the best-documented tools in this space (Ada, Babylon, NHS 111, and comparative multi-tool studies), three consistent findings emerge: accuracy is moderate and highly variable across presentation types; life-threatening presentations are still missed by even the better-performing tools; and where tools err, they tend to err toward over-caution rather than under-caution, though under-triage incidents do occur and — as the Babylon case shows — can be clinically serious even if statistically rare. No controlled, peer-reviewed comparative study of CarePrior's specific problem (AI-assisted *appointment* intake prioritisation, as opposed to diagnostic symptom checking or ED self-triage) was located during this review; this is itself part of the evidentiary gap discussed in Section 6, and is flagged here rather than papered over.

## 4. Explainable AI, Human-in-the-Loop Review, and Automation Bias

CarePrior's requirement that every AI-influenced recommendation be reviewed, accepted, or overridden (with a mandatory override reason) by a human before any action follows is grounded in a well-established concern in the clinical-AI literature: automation bias.

**Automation bias as a documented phenomenon.** Automation bias — the tendency of a human operator to over-rely on an automated system's output, including failing to notice or act on contradictory evidence — has been studied in clinical decision support since at least the early 2010s. A frequently cited paper, "Automation bias – a hidden issue for clinical decision support system use" (PubMed, Goddard, Roudsari & Wyatt) [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21335682/], frames the problem as deference to system output occurring "even when contradictory evidence is available." A secondary source reports that in one documented medication-related decision-support study, an incorrect system flag was associated with a 56.9% increase in physician prescribing errors; this specific figure could not be traced to a primary source within this search and is flagged as a secondary claim of moderate strength. A more recent Bowtie risk-modelling analysis maps the causal pathways by which automation bias in healthcare AI can lead to patient harm (ScienceDirect, "Exploring the risks of automation bias in healthcare artificial intelligence applications: A Bowtie analysis," 2024) [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666449624000410], and related work on electronic prescribing documents automation bias as a recognised, non-hypothetical risk in deployed systems (PMC, "Automation bias in electronic prescribing") [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5356416/].

**Explainability as a partial mitigation.** The XAI literature converges on the view that clinicians are more willing to trust, adopt, and critically interrogate an AI output when it is accompanied by an interpretable rationale rather than a bare label; contextual, actionable explanations are reported to improve confidence and support shared decision-making (arXiv, "Explainable AI improves task performance in human-AI collaboration," 2024) [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08271]. The widely cited working definition of interpretability from Doshi-Velez and Kim — "the ability to explain or to present in understandable terms to a human" — remains the field's most common starting point despite being criticised (including by Lipton) for lacking mathematical precision (via Marcinkevičs & Vogt, *WIREs Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery*, 2023) [https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/widm.1493]. A caution directly relevant to CarePrior's design discipline: explanations that "look logical and sound clinical" can produce *false* comprehension in a reviewer, so an explanation interface alone, without a structural safeguard, is not sufficient to prevent automation bias (general synthesis of XAI clinical-commentary sources rather than a single primary study, flagged accordingly).

**Human-in-the-loop as a structural safeguard.** CarePrior's architecture goes beyond "show the user an explanation": deterministic outcomes are structurally non-overridable by the AI layer, and human review of every AI-influenced case is mandatory, not optional. This mirrors a broader governance argument that accountability requires active human oversight built into the workflow — audit trails and explanations are necessary but insufficient without an enforced human decision point (industry commentary, e.g. Censinet, "Trust But Verify" and "The Audit Trail Imperative"; vendor/consultancy sources, not peer-reviewed, included because they specifically address audit-trail design patterns for clinical AI) [https://censinet.com/perspectives/trust-but-verify-building-accountability-healthcare-ai-systems] [https://censinet.com/perspectives/audit-trail-imperative-documentation-standards-healthcare-ai]. A peer-reviewed direction is represented by work on auditable, source-verified clinical AI decision-support frameworks that build data provenance directly into the architecture rather than bolting it on afterward (PMC, "An auditable and source-verified framework for clinical AI decision support") [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12913532/]. CarePrior's append-only audit log, recording every step from AI extraction through rule evaluation to human accept/override decisions with mandatory reasons, implements this "provenance built into the architecture" principle rather than an after-the-fact compliance log.

**Trust and adoption.** Survey evidence on trust cuts both ways: a widely cited 2023 consumer survey finding suggests only around 10% of US patients are comfortable with an AI-generated diagnosis, while aggregated physician-side survey data (cited figures around 72–76%) shows most physicians doubting chatbots can meet patients' emotional or diagnostic-nuance needs (both figures via industry survey-aggregation sources rather than independently retrieved primary instruments, and should be treated as indicative). A more rigorous qualitative source, a 2025 interview study of primary healthcare professionals and patients in Sweden, frames trust in AI-based triage as determined by transparency, perceived competence, and the visible presence of a human safety net (*Frontiers in Digital Health*, 2025) [https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2025.1565080/full] — supporting the argument that mandatory human review is plausibly a trust and adoption enabler, not only a safety control.

## 5. Adaptive/Branching Questionnaires versus Static Intake Forms

CarePrior's adaptive questioning engine — which asks a bounded set of follow-up questions (maximum nine), branching on prior answers, so that, for example, only patients who report chest discomfort are asked about radiation to the arm or associated sweating — has a direct precedent in the patient-reported-outcome-measurement (PROM) literature on computer-adaptive testing (CAT), as well as an emerging literature on adaptive-questioning conversational symptom checkers.

**Evidence for adaptive over static instruments.** A study comparing patient perceptions of computer-adaptive measures against static questionnaires found a clear preference for the adaptive approach, described as more engaging and better matched to individual situations (*Journal of Patient-Reported Outcomes*, "Do patients consider computer-adaptive measures more appropriate than static questionnaires?", 2019) [https://jpro.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41687-019-0096-3]. The same source documents a concrete failure mode of static forms: patients are frequently asked a high proportion of items inappropriate to their situation — e.g. a patient with difficulty walking short distances being asked about strenuous sports — which does not improve measurement precision and can reduce willingness to complete the form accurately. Earlier quantitative work comparing computer-adaptive against static short forms for measuring depressive symptoms found CAT outperformed static short forms on measurement-efficiency criteria in almost every comparison (*Quality of Life Research*, 2009) [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11136-009-9560-5]. This is PROM measurement-science evidence rather than triage-specific, but it supports the general proposition that branching, response-conditioned question selection extracts more clinically useful information per question than a fixed form.

**Evidence specific to conversational/symptom-checker contexts.** More directly relevant is recent work on adaptive questioning within conversational clinical intake. A pre-print evaluation of an adaptive-questioning symptom checker against standardised clinical vignettes (medRxiv, 2025) [https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.21.25333628.full.pdf], and its associated peer review (PMC) [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12551931/], evaluate the premise that asking the right follow-up questions is a necessary precursor to safe triage advice, prior to any diagnostic-mapping step. Separately, research on optimal question selection from a large question bank in conversational psychiatric intake found the benefit of adaptive selection over a fixed sequence grows as patient response style becomes more guarded or terse — i.e. branching matters most exactly when raw free text is least informative on its own (arXiv, "Optimal Question Selection...for Clinical Field Recovery in Conversational Psychiatric Intake") [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22067]. This is a strong conceptual match for CarePrior's adaptive questioning engine, which exists precisely to recover structured, patient-confirmed answers from sparse or ambiguous free text that AI extraction alone cannot fully resolve.

**A caution on evidence strength.** Much of the CAT/PROM literature addresses outcome measurement rather than acute triage decision-making, and the conversational-symptom-checker adaptive-questioning literature is very recent (2024–2025) and includes pre-prints rather than only peer-reviewed studies. The evidence supports the proposition that adaptive branching improves information yield and patient experience relative to static forms; it does not yet constitute a mature, triage-specific evidence base establishing that adaptive questioning measurably improves downstream triage *accuracy* (as opposed to information quality) in a controlled comparison. This gap is itself one of the research questions CarePrior's evaluation should speak to (Section 7).

## 6. The Gap in the Literature and CarePrior's Contribution

Drawing the four preceding sections together, four distinct properties recur separately across different parts of the literature, but no system located in this review combines all four for the *appointment intake prioritisation* problem specifically:

1. **Never-overridable deterministic safety rules.** Well established in emergency triage science (MTS, ESI, ATS) but absent from any booking system reviewed in Section 1 (no clinical reasoning at all) and only partially present in AI symptom checkers (Section 3), which typically produce a single blended recommendation with no structural guarantee that a documented high-risk pattern cannot be masked by an otherwise "reassuring" AI read of the free text — precisely the failure mode implicated in the Babylon Health chest-pain case.

2. **AI-assisted adaptive questioning.** Present, in early and promising form, in recent conversational symptom checkers (Section 5) and supported analogically by the mature CAT/PROM literature, but typically coupled to a diagnostic or self-triage output, not to a bounded, structured hand-off into a separate deterministic safety layer.

3. **Mandatory, explainable human review before any action.** Well justified by the automation-bias and XAI literature (Section 4) given known clinician over-reliance on automated recommendations and the demonstrated moderate-and-variable accuracy of AI triage tools (Section 3). Most deployed symptom checkers reviewed here (Ada, Babylon, NHS 111, Buoy, K Health) are designed for direct patient self-triage with no mandatory clinician-in-the-loop step before the patient acts on the advice — the entire design premise of "self-triage" is that the patient, not a clinician, decides what to do next.

4. **A full, append-only audit trail linking AI extraction, rule evaluation, and human decision.** Discussed as a governance *aspiration* in recent commentary (Section 4), but no widely documented, evaluated system in the appointment-intake space was found to implement it end-to-end as a structural (not merely logging) feature, where the audit record is itself part of what makes the human-review step meaningful and reviewable after the fact.

The compound gap, in other words, is not that any one property is novel in isolation — each has meaningful precedent — but that the literature does not document a system combining all four specifically for the *pre-clinical appointment-intake prioritisation* problem, as distinct from (a) pure scheduling logistics with no clinical content, and (b) patient-facing self-triage tools that encourage the patient to act on the AI's read of their situation without a mandatory clinician checkpoint. AI symptom checkers that attempt diagnosis (Ada, WebMD, Babylon, K Health, Buoy) inherit the accuracy and safety limitations documented in Section 3 and carry liability and scope-creep risk, since they reason about *what the patient has*, not merely *how urgently a clinician should look at what was reported*. Pure booking systems (Section 1) avoid that risk by avoiding clinical reasoning altogether, at the cost of doing no prioritisation at all.

CarePrior's design directly targets this gap. The deterministic rule engine, versioned and modelled on the explicit-discriminator logic of MTS/ESI/ATS, evaluates only patient-confirmed structured answers and can assign "Immediate Human Review" outcomes the AI layer is architecturally prevented from downgrading — directly addressing the Babylon-style failure mode. The adaptive questioning engine (maximum nine questions, branching on prior answers) recovers the structured signal the rule engine needs from sparse or ambiguous free text, consistent with the CAT/PROM and conversational-intake evidence that branching outperforms static forms without over-questioning low-risk patients. AI failure or low confidence routes explicitly to "Manual Review Required" rather than defaulting to "Routine," a direct structural response to the automation-bias literature's finding that an uncertain AI signal must never pass silently as reassurance. Every recommendation requires mandatory staff accept/override with a recorded reason before any consequence follows, addressing both the automation-bias literature's call for enforced human oversight and the trust literature's finding that visible human safety nets support trust. Finally, the append-only audit log makes the entire chain — AI extraction, adaptive answers, rule evaluation, human decision and reason — reviewable after the fact, the governance property recent commentary identifies as necessary but rarely implemented structurally.

CarePrior's boundary is worth stating honestly here: it does not diagnose, prescribe, or auto-act, and is not evaluated against ED triage performance, because it is not an ED tool — it is scoped to primary-care appointment-request prioritisation, a narrower and lower-stakes problem than ED triage or diagnostic symptom checking. That narrower scope is what makes combining all four properties tractable for a solo-developer capstone prototype, and why the evaluation proposed in Section 7 is feasible within the project's timeframe.

## 7. Research Questions This Literature Motivates

The literature reviewed above motivates a structured, comparative evaluation of CarePrior against simpler alternatives, rather than an evaluation of CarePrior in isolation. The following research questions are proposed, framed around a hybrid-vs-static/rule-only/AI-only comparison:

1. **RQ1 (Safety floor).** With the AI interpretation layer disabled, does the deterministic rule engine, evaluated on patient-confirmed structured answers alone, correctly and consistently flag documented high-risk presentation patterns (e.g., chest discomfort with radiation or diaphoresis) as "Immediate Human Review," at a rate comparable to or better than the under-triage rates documented for ESI (~10.7%) and ATS (Sections 2–3) — and does this rate remain stable regardless of the AI layer's output?

2. **RQ2 (Information yield of adaptive questioning).** Compared to a static, fixed-order questionnaire covering the same clinical content, does CarePrior's adaptive branching (bounded to nine questions) recover the structured information the rule engine needs in fewer questions, with fewer irrelevant questions asked of low-risk patients, and higher patient-reported ease of completion — mirroring the CAT-versus-static-form findings in Section 5?

3. **RQ3 (Hybrid vs AI-only).** Compared to an AI-only configuration (prioritisation decided solely by AI confidence/severity output, with no rule engine or mandatory override), does the hybrid configuration reduce the rate at which a genuinely high-risk case is under-prioritised — the failure mode implicated in the Babylon Health case — without materially increasing over-triage beyond what is already an accepted, deliberate safety trade-off in the NHS 111 and Ada literature (Section 3)?

4. **RQ4 (Hybrid vs rule-only).** Compared to a rule-only configuration (deterministic rules evaluated against raw patient answers, with no AI-assisted adaptive questioning), does the AI-assisted questioning layer reduce the incidence of "insufficient information" or contradictory-answer states that would otherwise force escalation due to incomplete intake rather than genuine clinical uncertainty?

5. **RQ5 (Automation bias / reviewer behaviour).** When staff reviewers see the full reasoning chain (AI extraction, adaptive answers, rule evaluation) versus a bare priority label, does visible explanation measurably reduce uncritical acceptance of incorrect AI-influenced recommendations, consistent with the XAI literature's claim that contextual explanation mitigates, but does not eliminate, automation bias (Section 4)?

6. **RQ6 (Auditability as an evaluation criterion).** Does the append-only audit log, reconstructed post hoc for a sample of cases, give a reviewer sufficient information to independently reproduce and justify the system's recommendation without consulting the live system — operationalising "auditability" as a testable property rather than an assumed one?

These are deliberately ablation-style comparisons (hybrid vs AI-only, hybrid vs rule-only, hybrid vs static-form) because the gap identified in Section 6 is a gap in *combinations* of properties, not any single property; the strongest evaluation of CarePrior's contribution isolates what each component adds over simpler configurations, using the same class of vignette-based methodology as the Semigran, Ada, and JMIR AI comparative studies reviewed in Section 3.
